Both robotic and conventional knee replacement use the same approved implants and aim for the same result — a pain-free knee that lets you walk, climb stairs, and squat normally again. The real difference is *how* the bone cuts are made, not what you end up with. Here's a plain-language comparison to help you and your surgeon decide which fits your knee.

"My knee surgery is booked next month — should I pay extra for robotic?" It's the single most common question patients ask once they've decided to go ahead with [knee replacement surgery](/procedures/knee-replacement-surgery). There's no universal right answer — but there is a right answer for your specific anatomy, age, and activity level. This article walks through both techniques honestly, including where robotic genuinely helps and where it doesn't change the outcome.

## What Is Conventional Knee Replacement?

Conventional ("traditional") knee replacement is the world standard, refined over more than three decades of use. The surgeon uses mechanical jigs, visual landmarks, and manual instruments to align and cut the bone, then fits the implant components by hand.

It works. [Total knee replacement](/procedures/knee-replacement-surgery) is one of the most predictable operations in orthopaedics, and Dr. Dheeraj Dubay's own outcomes across 35,000+ joint replacements reflect that track record. Conventional technique is suitable for the vast majority of patients — particularly those with reasonably standard knee anatomy and no major prior trauma to the joint.

## What Is Robotic Knee Replacement?

[Robotic knee replacement](/procedures/robotic-knee-replacement) doesn't replace the surgeon — it constrains and guides what the surgeon's hand can do. Before surgery, a CT scan builds a 3D model of your specific knee, which becomes a personalised cutting plan. On surgery day, small trackers are placed on the bone, and the robotic arm keeps Dr. Dubay's instruments inside the pre-planned boundaries — physically preventing a cut outside the plan. The system also gives live feedback on soft-tissue tension and balance as the knee moves through its range of motion.

The robot cannot operate independently at any point. Dr. Dubay makes every surgical decision and performs every cut; the robotic arm simply won't let the instrument stray outside the plan he's already approved.

## Who Should Choose Robotic?

Robotic guidance earns its premium in specific situations:

- **Unusual anatomy** — a knee with a prior fracture, significant deformity, or previous surgery, where the landmarks a surgeon would normally use for alignment are distorted
- **Very active patients under 60** who want the most even implant wear possible, to maximise how long the implant lasts
- **Bilateral knee replacement candidates**, where consistent, repeatable precision on both knees in one session matters
- **Patients who value the data** — the pre-op plan and real-time feedback — and can comfortably afford the premium

## Who's Fine With Conventional?

- Standard knee anatomy with no major prior trauma
- Cost-sensitive patients — and there's no shame in this. For most patients, a well-performed conventional replacement gets an equivalent outcome at a lower cost
- Patients of a high-volume, experienced surgeon, where years of repetition substitute for robotic guidance in achieving consistent alignment

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is robotic surgery safer than conventional?**
Both have comparable safety profiles in experienced hands. Robotic guidance adds precision to implant placement — it isn't primarily a safety feature, and neither technique is inherently riskier than the other when performed by a high-volume surgeon.

**Will insurance cover robotic knee replacement?**
The surgery itself is typically covered under standard knee replacement policies. Whether the robotic technology premium is covered varies by insurer and policy — check with your TPA before committing. Dr. Dubay's clinic team can help with pre-authorisation either way.

**How long do the implants last?**
Modern knee implants are designed to last two decades or more with either technique — the implant materials themselves are the same regardless of how the cuts were made.

**Can the robot make a mistake on its own?**
No — it's a guidance and constraint tool, not an autonomous surgeon. See above: Dr. Dubay is in control throughout.
