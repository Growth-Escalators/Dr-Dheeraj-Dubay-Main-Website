For decades, total hip replacement was the only real answer for severe hip arthritis. Hip resurfacing — a bone-preserving alternative that caps rather than replaces the femoral head — has since carved out a role for younger, highly active patients. Here's an honest look at who each approach actually suits.

## What Is Total Hip Replacement (THR)?

[Total hip replacement](/procedures/hip-replacement-surgery) replaces both sides of the joint — the femoral head (ball) and the acetabulum (socket). The femoral head and part of the neck of the thigh bone are removed and replaced with a metal or ceramic ball on a stem inserted into the bone; the socket is fitted with a new metal cup. It has been refined for more than 50 years and remains the gold-standard operation for the vast majority of patients needing hip surgery.

## What Is Hip Resurfacing?

Hip resurfacing takes a different approach: instead of removing the femoral head, the surgeon shaves and caps its worn surface with a metal covering, while the socket is separately resurfaced with a cup. The femoral neck and most of the natural bone of the head are preserved. The Birmingham Hip Resurfacing (BHR) implant is the most widely studied resurfacing system on the market, with a meaningful long-term track record.

## Who's a Candidate for Resurfacing?

- Male patients — outcomes have historically been more consistent in men than women
- Generally younger, more active patients
- Strong, normal-density bone — significant osteoporosis is a contraindication
- An active lifestyle, including runners and sports participants
- Severe hip arthritis in someone who is otherwise in good general health

## Who Should Choose Total Hip Replacement?

- Women — outcomes have historically been more reliable with THR
- Patients over roughly 60
- Anyone with osteoporosis or weaker bone
- Severe deformity or hip dysplasia
- Anyone for whom bone preservation isn't the deciding priority

## The Metal-on-Metal Concern, Addressed Honestly

Early-generation resurfacing implants used metal-on-metal bearings, and some patients experienced elevated metal ion levels in their blood as a result — this is real, published history, not a fringe concern. Implant materials and designs have moved on since; if resurfacing is on the table for you, ask specifically what bearing surface is being used and what the monitoring protocol looks like. An annual blood test in the early years after resurfacing is standard practice regardless of implant generation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can you go back to running after hip resurfacing?**
Many patients do return to running and higher-impact sport after resurfacing, which is one of its main appeals for younger, active patients — though this depends on individual healing and your surgeon's clearance.

**Will either implant set off airport security?**
Possibly — metal implants can trigger metal detectors. It's worth carrying an implant identification card, which your surgical team can provide.

**Is the recovery time different?**
Broadly similar for both — most patients are looking at a 6–12 week window to return to most normal activities, with full recovery continuing for several months after that.

**Is one more expensive than the other in India?**
Costs are broadly comparable, though the specific figures depend on implant choice and hospital package.
