Many patients arrive assuming they need a "total" knee replacement when a partial replacement — resurfacing just the damaged part of the knee — may be enough. Partial procedures preserve more of your natural joint and typically recover faster, but they're only right for a specific group of patients. Here's how to tell which one applies to you.

## Anatomy Refresher: The Knee's Three Compartments

The knee joint has three separate compartments: the medial (inner side), the lateral (outer side), and the patellofemoral (behind the kneecap). Arthritis frequently starts in just one compartment — most often the medial — while the other two remain relatively healthy. [Total knee replacement](/procedures/knee-replacement-surgery) resurfaces all three; [partial knee replacement](/procedures/partial-knee-replacement) resurfaces only the one that's actually damaged.

## What Is Partial Knee Replacement?

Also called unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), partial knee replacement resurfaces only the affected compartment, leaving healthy bone, cartilage, and ligaments untouched. The incision is noticeably smaller than for a total replacement, and because less bone and tissue are disturbed, recovery is typically quicker.

## What Is Total Knee Replacement?

All three compartments are resurfaced. The incision is larger, and full functional recovery generally takes longer — commonly 3 to 6 months. It remains the gold-standard, most predictable option for advanced, multi-compartment arthritis, and it's the right call whenever damage isn't confined to a single area.

## Who Qualifies for Partial Knee Replacement?

**Good candidates typically have:**
- Arthritis confirmed as isolated to one compartment on MRI or X-ray
- An intact ACL (anterior cruciate ligament)
- A stable knee with reasonable range of motion
- No significant deformity
- A healthy-range body weight

**Partial replacement is usually not appropriate for:**
- Inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis — total replacement is the better option here
- Multi-compartment disease, where more than one part of the knee is already damaged

## Why Partial Has a Higher Revision Rate — and Why That's Not Necessarily a Deal-Breaker

The compartments that weren't replaced in a partial knee replacement can still develop arthritis over time — sometimes a decade or more later — which is the main reason lifetime revision rates run higher for partial than total. The good news: a partial-to-total conversion is a well-established procedure if that day comes. For many younger, active patients, getting 10–15 genuinely good years out of a smaller, faster-recovering surgery is a reasonable trade-off — even with a real chance of a second surgery down the line.

## The Doctor's Role in This Decision

Don't accept a quick verdict without a proper look — the "partial vs total" call needs a detailed X-ray and MRI assessment, not just a glance at your knee. If a surgeon recommends total replacement without ever discussing whether partial applies to you, it's worth getting a second opinion. An experienced surgeon should be equally comfortable performing either.
