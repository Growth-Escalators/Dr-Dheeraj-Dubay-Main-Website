# Article 2 — When Should You Consider Knee Replacement?

**Slug:** `when-to-consider-knee-replacement`
**Meta title:** When Should You Consider Knee Replacement Surgery? 7 Warning Signs
**Meta description:** 7 signs your knee pain has reached the surgery stage. When physiotherapy + medication isn't enough, what to expect from a specialist consultation.
**Target keywords:** when to get knee replacement, knee replacement signs, do i need knee surgery, knee pain when to see doctor, severe knee pain treatment
**Estimated word count:** 1,500

## Structure

### H1: When Should You Consider Knee Replacement Surgery?

**Hook:**
Knee replacement isn't the first treatment any orthopedic surgeon recommends. It's reserved for cases where conservative options — exercise, medication, physiotherapy, weight loss — have stopped working. So how do you know you've reached that point? Here are 7 signs.

### H2: First — the conservative ladder
Before surgery is even on the table, you'll have tried:
- Weight management
- Quadriceps strengthening exercises
- NSAIDs (paracetamol, ibuprofen) under doctor supervision
- Physiotherapy
- Intra-articular injections (steroid, hyaluronic acid)
- Activity modification (cushioned shoes, avoiding squatting/stairs)

If you've tried 3+ of these for 6+ months without lasting relief, this article is for you.

### H2: 7 signs it's time to consult a knee specialist
1. **Pain wakes you up at night** — bone-on-bone friction doesn't quiet down with rest
2. **You can't walk 500 metres without stopping** — measurable functional decline
3. **Pain medication isn't lasting** — daily NSAIDs that used to work now don't
4. **You've stopped doing things you love** — gardening, temple visits, family outings
5. **Knee gives way without warning** — instability is dangerous and points to ligament/cartilage damage
6. **Visible deformity** — bow legs or knock knees getting worse
7. **X-ray shows severe arthritis** — Grade 3 or 4 on Kellgren-Lawrence scale `[VERIFY]`

### H2: What "Grade 3/4 arthritis" looks like on X-ray
- Grade 0: normal
- Grade 1: mild changes, joint space looks normal
- Grade 2: small bone spurs
- Grade 3: clear joint space narrowing, multiple spurs
- Grade 4: bone-on-bone, complete cartilage loss
*(Surgery typically discussed at Grade 3, recommended at Grade 4)*

### H2: Age myths
- **"I'm too young for knee replacement"** — modern implants last 20–25 years; replacement at 55 means revision around 75, which is still doable. Living in pain for 20 years isn't a better option.
- **"I'm too old"** — patients up to 85+ have safe outcomes when overall health is good. Cardiac fitness matters more than age.

### H2: What happens at a knee specialist consultation
- Detailed pain history (when, where, how often)
- Physical exam (range of motion, stability, alignment)
- X-ray review (often you'll be asked to get new X-rays in standing position)
- Discussion of options — conservative path may still be tried
- If surgery: implant choice, technique (robotic vs conventional), recovery plan

### H2: How to prepare for the consultation
- Bring existing X-rays + reports
- List medications you've tried + how long
- Write down your top 3 daily limitations (e.g., "can't squat for puja")
- Bring a family member (decisions involve them too)

# Article 3 — Knee Replacement Recovery: Week by Week

**Slug:** `knee-replacement-recovery-week-by-week`
**Meta title:** Knee Replacement Recovery: Day-by-Day, Week-by-Week Timeline
**Meta description:** Realistic recovery timeline after knee replacement — what you'll feel in week 1, 2, 4, 8, 12. Walking, driving, returning to work, full recovery.
**Target keywords:** knee replacement recovery time, knee surgery recovery week by week, after knee replacement, knee replacement day 1, knee replacement timeline
**Estimated word count:** 2,000

## Structure

### H1: Knee Replacement Recovery: A Realistic Week-by-Week Guide

**Hook:**
Recovery from knee replacement is more predictable than most patients fear. With the Zero Technique pioneered by Dr. Dheeraj Dubay, patients walk within 24 hours of surgery. Here's a realistic, no-sugarcoating timeline of what to expect from Day 1 through Month 6.

### H2: Day 0 (surgery day)
- Anaesthesia: spinal block + sedation (most common)
- Surgery time: 60–120 minutes
- Recovery room: 2–3 hours
- Pain level: 4–6 out of 10 (managed with IV painkillers)
- You'll be in your room by evening

### H2: Day 1
- Physiotherapist comes to your bedside
- First standing + a few steps with walker
- IV painkillers continue
- Bandage check, drain removed
- Most patients are surprised they can stand

### H2: Days 2–3
- Walking 20–30 metres on flat surface with walker
- Toilet without help (with grab bar)
- Pain transitioning from IV to oral
- Climbing 2–3 stairs supervised

### H2: Discharge (Day 3–5 for most patients)
- Walking 50+ metres independently with walker
- Knee bend at 80–90° `[VERIFY]`
- Going home with pain meds + a follow-up appointment
- Home setup checklist: bedside commode, elevated bed, no scatter rugs

### H2: Week 1 at home
- Daily exercises 4–5 times (physio gives you a sheet)
- Ice + elevation 3–4 times a day
- Some patients switch from walker to cane by end of week 1
- DON'T: sit cross-legged, squat, lift heavy weights

### H2: Week 2
- Stitches/staples removed (Day 10–14)
- Most patients now walking around the house freely
- Knee bend progressing to 100–110°
- Driving — NO (for the operated knee — wait week 6)

### H2: Week 4
- Light household chores
- Cane optional indoors
- Stairs becoming routine
- Back to OPD follow-up

### H2: Week 6
- Most desk jobs OK
- Driving an automatic car is usually fine (consult doctor)
- Walking 1+ km daily
- Knee bend should be 120°+

### H2: Week 8–12
- Most patients are at 85–90% of full function
- Pre-surgery activities resuming (puja, temple, light gardening)
- Long-distance travel OK
- Swelling fully gone

### H2: Month 4–6: full recovery
- Implant fully integrated
- Most patients can squat (low chair, support)
- Return to swimming, brisk walking, cycling
- Long-term: avoid running on hard surfaces, no contact sports

### H2: Red flags during recovery (when to call the doctor)
- Sudden severe swelling (could be DVT — blood clot)
- Fever > 100°F
- Wound oozing or smelling bad
- Pain that suddenly worsens after improving
- Difficulty breathing — go to ER

### H2: How to recover faster
- Strict adherence to physio exercises
- Maintain weight
- Don't push too hard too early
- Sleep on your back with a pillow under the calf (not the knee)
- Stay hydrated, eat protein-rich food

# Article 4 — Hip Replacement vs Hip Resurfacing

**Slug:** `hip-replacement-vs-hip-resurfacing`
**Meta title:** Hip Replacement vs Hip Resurfacing — Which Is Better for You?
**Meta description:** Comparing total hip replacement and hip resurfacing: who qualifies, bone preservation, recovery, longevity, activity levels.
**Target keywords:** hip replacement vs resurfacing, total hip vs resurfacing, hip resurfacing india, birmingham hip resurfacing, who needs hip replacement
**Estimated word count:** 1,600

## Structure

### H1: Hip Replacement vs Hip Resurfacing: Choosing the Right Approach

**Hook:**
For a long time, total hip replacement was the only answer for severe hip arthritis. Hip resurfacing — a bone-preserving alternative — has gained ground for younger, more active patients. Here's how to decide between them.

### H2: What is total hip replacement (THR)?
- Both the femoral head AND the acetabulum (socket) are replaced
- The femoral head + part of the neck are cut and replaced with a metal/ceramic ball on a stem
- 30+ years of refinement, gold standard for most patients

### H2: What is hip resurfacing?
- Only the worn surface of the femoral head is shaved + capped
- Bone-preserving — you keep your femoral neck and most of the head
- Birmingham hip resurfacing (BHR) is the most widely-studied implant
- 20+ year track record `[VERIFY]`

### H2: Side-by-side
| | THR | Resurfacing |
|---|---|---|
| Bone preserved | Less | More — keeps femoral neck |
| Best age range | Any | <60 typically `[VERIFY]` |
| Best for women | Yes | Caution — smaller bones may not fit standard sizes |
| Activity level after | Moderate–high | Higher (some return to running) `[VERIFY]` |
| Revision rate (10-yr) | <5% | 5–8% historically `[VERIFY]` |
| Surgery complexity | Standard | Higher — fewer surgeons trained |

### H2: Who's a candidate for resurfacing?
- Male patients (better outcomes than female on average)
- Under 60
- Strong, normal-density bone (no severe osteoporosis)
- Active lifestyle — runners, sports
- Severe hip arthritis but otherwise healthy

### H2: Who should choose THR?
- Women (better outcomes historically)
- Patients over 60
- Osteoporosis or weak bone
- Severe deformity / hip dysplasia
- Anyone where bone-preservation isn't a priority

### H2: The metal-on-metal concern
- Older resurfacing implants used metal-on-metal bearings → some patients had elevated blood ion levels
- Modern resurfacing uses improved materials with much lower wear `[VERIFY current materials]`
- Annual blood test in the first few years is standard

- Honest about cases where THR is the safer call

### H2: FAQ

# Article 5 — Partial vs Total Knee Replacement

**Slug:** `partial-vs-total-knee-replacement`
**Meta title:** Partial vs Total Knee Replacement — Which Do You Need?
**Meta description:** Partial knee replacement preserves more bone but isn't right for everyone. Compare partial and total knee replacement, candidacy, recovery, longevity.
**Target keywords:** partial vs total knee replacement, partial knee replacement india, unicompartmental knee replacement, when to choose partial knee surgery
**Estimated word count:** 1,500

## Structure

### H1: Partial vs Total Knee Replacement: Which Surgery Do You Need?

**Hook:**
Many patients arrive convinced they need a "total" knee replacement, when in reality a partial knee replacement may be enough. Partial procedures preserve more of your natural knee, recover faster, and feel more normal — but they're not for everyone. Here's the breakdown.

### H2: Anatomy refresher
- The knee has 3 compartments: medial (inner), lateral (outer), patellofemoral (kneecap)
- Arthritis often starts in ONE compartment, especially the medial
- Total replacement replaces all three; partial replaces only the affected one

### H2: What is partial knee replacement?
- Also called unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA)
- Only the damaged compartment is resurfaced
- 50% smaller incision
- 50% faster recovery typical `[VERIFY]`

### H2: What is total knee replacement?
- All 3 compartments resurfaced
- Larger incision
- Longer recovery (3–6 months for full function)
- Gold standard for advanced multi-compartment arthritis

### H2: Who qualifies for partial?
- ✓ Arthritis isolated to ONE compartment (proven on MRI/X-ray)
- ✓ Intact ACL (cruciate ligament)
- ✓ Stable knee with good range of motion
- ✓ No significant deformity
- ✓ Weight in healthy range
- ✗ Inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid) — total is better
- ✗ Multi-compartment disease

### H2: Side-by-side
| | Partial | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Incision size | 3–4 inches | 6–8 inches |
| Hospital stay | 1–2 days | 3–4 days |
| Walking | Same day | 24 hours |
| Full recovery | 6 weeks | 12 weeks |
| Feel of knee | More natural | More mechanical |
| Lifetime revision rate | 10–15% (higher) `[VERIFY]` | 5–10% |
| Suitable for active people? | Excellent | Good |

### H2: Why partial has a HIGHER revision rate
- The other compartments can develop arthritis later (10+ years)
- Partial revisions can be converted to total
- It's still a worthwhile trade-off for younger patients who get 10–15 great years before any potential revision

### H2: The doctor's role in this decision
- Don't trust a quick verdict — needs detailed X-ray + MRI assessment
- Get a second opinion if the first surgeon recommends total without discussing partial
- An experienced surgeon should be comfortable with both

### H2: Dr. Dheeraj Dubay's approach
- Performs both partial and total
- Will recommend partial when patient qualifies — preserves bone, faster recovery
- Quote on his philosophy `[doctor to provide]`
